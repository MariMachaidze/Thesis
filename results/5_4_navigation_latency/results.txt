================================================================================
Section 5.4 — Robot Navigation Latency
Gesture-Based Teleoperation of a Sphero Mobile Robot
================================================================================

Dataset
-------
The analysis is based on 275 arrived trials extracted from
the recording sessions stored in bags/thesis_final/. Each trial
corresponds to one user pointing gesture that was successfully
committed by the gating logic (t_start) together with the
subsequent first continuous in-square run of the Sphero robot.
Latency is defined per trial as

    latency_s = t_min_distance - t_start

where t_min_distance is the detection sample at which the robot
attained its minimum distance to the user's pointed coordinate.
Travel distance for each trial is the Euclidean distance, in
centimetres, between the previous trial's pointed coordinate (the
robot's approximate parking position) and the current trial's
pointed coordinate, chained within each source bag. The first
trial of each bag has no prior parking position and is therefore
excluded from the distance-based analyses; the latency
descriptives use all 275 trials while the regression and
binned breakdown use 259 trials with a defined
travel distance (mean 8.9 cm, max
32.3 cm).

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. Overall navigation responsiveness
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Across all 275 trials the mean end-to-end navigation
latency was 6.93 s (SD = 4.97 s;
median = 5.57 s; min = 0.13 s;
max = 32.62 s). The 25th and 75th percentiles were
3.93 s and 8.70 s respectively, and the
95th percentile sat at 15.38 s. The median falling
below the mean indicates a mildly right-skewed distribution, with
the bulk of trials resolving quickly and a smaller tail of slower
arrivals that pull the mean upwards.

Decomposed into its two natural phases the latency comprises
a transit phase of 6.60 s on average
(t_start -> t_entry, the time taken for the robot to first enter
the target square) and a settling phase of 0.34 s
on average (t_entry -> t_min_distance, the additional time the
controller spends fine-tuning the in-square pose). The transit
phase accounts for almost the entire end-to-end latency, while
the in-square settling phase is short and consistent across
trials.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2. Latency variation
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Cumulative breakdown of the latency distribution:

    Within 1 s :   1.8 %
    Within 2 s :   6.9 %
    Within 3 s :  17.8 %
    Within 5 s :  40.7 %

The inter-quartile range of
4.77 s is comparable in magnitude to the median,
which means that trial-to-trial latency is variable rather than
tightly concentrated around a single value. As shown in the next
section, this variation is not explained by travel distance, so
the dominant source of variation must be sought in per-trial
factors such as initial heading misalignment of the Sphero,
intermittent corrective manoeuvres, and the time required for the
controller to satisfy the in-square dwell condition.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
3. Effect of travel distance on latency
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
A linear regression of latency on the trial's travel distance
(distance from the previous parking position to the current
pointed coordinate, chained within each source bag) yields

    latency_s = 0.058 * distance_cm + 6.609
    Pearson r = +0.05,  R^2 = 0.00

The relationship is essentially flat. The fitted slope of
58 ms per centimetre is small and the regression
explains R^2 = 0.00 of the variance in latency, meaning that
how far the robot has to travel within the tabletop workspace is
not a meaningful predictor of how long the trial takes. Binned
per distance the picture is the same:

    bin (cm)      n     mean latency (s)
    0-10         180          7.11
    10-20         69          6.91
    20-30          9          8.85
    >30            1          8.68

The first two bins, which contain the overwhelming majority of
trials, are within a few hundred milliseconds of one another,
and the few trials in the longer-distance bins do not shift the
mean upwards in a way that would survive any reasonable
statistical test. The most plausible explanation is that on this
workspace scale (a few tens of centimetres) the time spent on
initial heading alignment, on corrective manoeuvres triggered by
the controller, and on satisfying the in-square dwell condition
dominates the actual transit time. The Sphero's heading is not
observed at t_start, so even a short-distance gesture can
require the robot to first rotate-in-place before any useful
translation begins.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
4. Smoothness and discussion
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Robot control responsiveness. The median end-to-end latency of
5.57 s reflects the rhythm of a deliberate
pointing-and-go interaction rather than a continuous teleoperation
loop: the user commits a gesture, the Sphero then has to physically
traverse the table, and only on arrival is the next gesture issued.
At this granularity the cause-effect link between gesture and
motion is preserved, but the responsiveness is bounded by the
robot's translation speed and not by the perceptual stack.

Effect of target distance. Within the tabletop workspace, travel
distance is essentially uncorrelated with latency. This is a
useful finding for interaction design: the user cannot anticipate
a shorter response by pointing closer to the robot, but the
worst-case latency does not blow up either. Even the slowest 5 %
of trials complete in under 15.4 s, bounding the
worst-case interaction time.

Smoothness of navigation. The settling phase contributes only
around 0.34 s on average, which indicates
that once the robot enters the target square it does not
oscillate or hunt around the goal. The bulk of the latency
budget is therefore spent on directed transit rather than on
end-of-trajectory correction, giving the motion the qualitative
character of a single committed manoeuvre rather than a
stop-and-correct sequence.

Suitability for interactive teleoperation. The pipeline is
appropriate for discrete pointing-and-go interaction on a
tabletop, where the user issues a gesture and waits a few
seconds for the robot to arrive before issuing the next one.
It is not suitable as-is for continuous-control teleoperation,
where sub-second responsiveness would be required. The lack of
a meaningful distance-latency coupling implies that the largest
gains in responsiveness would come from shortening the per-trial
overheads (initial heading alignment, controller corrections,
and in-square dwell) rather than from increasing translational
speed alone. The gesture-recognition and planning stages
upstream are not the bottleneck.
