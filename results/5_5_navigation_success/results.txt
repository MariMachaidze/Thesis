================================================================================
Section 5.5 -- Navigation Success Rate
Gesture-Based Teleoperation of a Sphero Mobile Robot
================================================================================

Dataset and success criterion
-----------------------------
The analysis uses the deduplicated aggregate of all per-session
results.csv files produced by thesis_ws/analyze_session.py
(bags/thesis_final/aggregate_results_deduped.csv), which contains
174 unique trials drawn from 16 recording sessions. A "trial" is one
continuous dwell of the user's pointing target on a single 10x10 cm
grid square that lasted at least the segmentation minimum dwell of
0.5 s; duplicate trial events with the same target square and start
time within 0.2 s were collapsed, preferring per-trial recordings
over overlapping exp_* recordings.

The success criterion is the one defined by the upstream pipeline:
a trial succeeds if the Sphero's centre eventually enters the
requested 10x10 cm target square after t_start. This is an
"eventual arrival" metric -- the robot may arrive after the user has
already moved their finger to the next square, but the binary
outcome only asks whether the requested cell was ever entered.
The criterion is therefore a geometric containment test against a
10 cm box, not a Euclidean radius around the square centre, and it
is independent of the per-trial navigation accuracy analysed in
Section 5.2.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. Overall success rate
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Across all 174 trials, the Sphero entered the requested target
square in 164 trials and failed to do so in 10 trials, giving an
overall success rate of 94.3 % (failure rate 5.7 %). Across the
16 sessions, individual success rates ranged from 78.6 %
(11/14 in trial_03_2026-05-14-17-06-32) to 100 % (8 sessions),
indicating that no single session drives the aggregate and that the
behaviour is reproducible across users and recording days.

The mean distance from the recorded robot position to the target-
square centre is 5.51 cm (SD 0.71) for arrived trials and 34.5 cm
(SD 17.9) for missed trials. The arrived population is bounded
above by the square half-diagonal (sqrt(2) * 5 cm = 7.07 cm), as it
must be -- by definition the robot is inside the 10 cm square at the
moment of arrival -- and the missed population sits an order of
magnitude further away. There is no overlap between the two
distributions, so the binary success label and a continuous distance
metric agree on every trial in the dataset.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2. Success rate by target region
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The workspace was partitioned into six rectangular regions by
splitting the target's x coordinate into Left / Centre / Right
thirds and its y coordinate into Near / Far halves. The per-region
success rates are:

    Near-Left    : 100.0 %  (14 / 14)
    Near-Centre  :  91.4 %  (32 / 35)
    Near-Right   :  84.6 %  (11 / 13)
    Far-Left     :  96.4 %  (27 / 28)
    Far-Centre   :  96.2 %  (51 / 53)
    Far-Right    :  93.5 %  (29 / 31)

The two halves of the workspace are essentially indistinguishable
(Near 91.6 %, Far 95.4 %), and all six regions sit in the
85--100 % band. The single lowest cell (Near-Right) is the smallest
sample (n = 13); a single additional miss in this cell would already
move it by ~7 percentage points, so the small per-region variation
is more easily explained by sampling noise than by a spatial
structural effect. There are no "dead zones" of the workspace at
which the robot is unable to arrive.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
3. Relationship between navigation error and success
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The outcome-vs-distance scatter (success_vs_error_scatter.png)
plots, for every trial, the Euclidean distance between the recorded
robot position and the centre of the target square, separated by
outcome. Three features are visible.

(a) All 164 arrived trials lie at or below 6.86 cm from the centre,
    which is the empirical upper bound of the square half-diagonal
    (7.07 cm). The arrived cluster is tightly concentrated around
    ~5.5 cm because the recorded position is captured at the
    moment the robot first crosses into the square -- typically
    near a square boundary rather than at its centre.

(b) All 10 missed trials lie far beyond the half-diagonal cut-off,
    with distances between roughly 12 cm and 67 cm. There is no
    "near miss" population just outside the square boundary.

(c) The conditional means (5.51 cm vs. 34.5 cm) differ by about a
    factor of six, and the two distributions are perfectly linearly
    separable in this projection. Success is therefore not a noisy
    thresholding of a continuous accuracy distribution; it
    partitions the trials into a populous low-error cluster and a
    small high-error tail.

The qualitative reading is that when the controller arrives at all,
it arrives accurately; when it fails, it fails decisively, with the
robot ending the trial window several target-square widths away
from the requested cell.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
4. Workspace heatmap
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The workspace heatmap (workspace_success_heatmap.png) bins the
target coordinates onto the same 10 cm grid used for the
experiment. The vast majority of populated cells are at or above
the 90 % success rate, and the few cells that drop lower contain
only a handful of trials each. The 0 % entry at (70, 10) is a
single trial that failed to arrive, not a systematic failure of the
far-right-near corner. No row, column, or diagonal of the heatmap
shows a coherent reduction in success rate, confirming the
region-level conclusion that the system is uniformly reliable
across the navigable workspace.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
5. Discussion
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Practical reliability. A 94.3 % within-square success rate over
174 trials, with the worst session still arriving on roughly four
out of five trials, is consistent with a system that is practically
reliable for coarse tabletop teleoperation. The user can issue a
sequence of pointing gestures and expect the robot to reach the
intended grid cell in essentially every trial. Failures are the
exception rather than a recurring frustration in interactive use.

Regions with lower success. The lowest per-region rate (Near-Right,
84.6 %) coincides with the smallest regional sample (n = 13), and
a single trial of difference shifts it by ~7 percentage points.
The Near vs. Far axis shows a 3.8-point difference (91.6 % vs.
95.4 %) that is consistent with the user occasionally pointing into
the body-near edge of the workspace where the camera sees the
projected finger at a shallow angle and the resulting target is
more variable. The Left/Right asymmetry is small (Left 97.6 %,
Right 91.4 %) and not concentrated in any one cell of the heatmap;
it is best read as residual sample variation. No region exhibits a
systematic geometric or controller-side failure mode.

Effect of localisation accuracy. The pointing localisation evaluated
in Section 5.1 produces single-shot errors on the order of a few
centimetres; given that the navigation target is the averaged
pointed coordinate, an upstream error of similar order is the
binding constraint on whether the robot can plausibly enter the
correct 10 cm square. The fact that 94 % of trials nevertheless
succeed at this binary criterion means that the pointing pipeline
typically resolves the correct grid cell even when its absolute
error is non-zero; failures in this section are therefore
concentrated at trials where the pointing estimate fell into a
neighbouring cell, not at trials where the controller failed to
track an accurately resolved target.

Observed behaviour during failed trials. Across the ten missed
trials, the distance between the last recorded robot position and
the requested square centre lies between ~12 cm and ~67 cm,
with no examples in the 5--10 cm "almost in the square" band. This
distribution is consistent with three qualitative failure modes
observed in the per-session map.png plots. (i) Off-by-one cell:
the controller drove the robot accurately to an adjacent square
because the averaged pointing target fell across a grid boundary;
this produces missed trials with end distances around 10--15 cm.
(ii) Pre-empted approach: the user moved their finger to the next
square before the robot finished its current approach, so the
controller abandoned the previous target while still several cells
away; this produces missed trials with end distances of 20--40 cm.
(iii) Tracking dropouts: occasional brief detection gaps caused the
last-known position to lag the robot's actual position by tens of
centimetres in the rare frames near the bag end. None of these
failure modes points to a defect of the navigation controller -- in
each case the controller did what was asked of it, but the
combination of upstream pointing noise, free-flowing protocol, and
detection coverage produced an end state outside the requested
cell.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
6. Summary
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Using the upstream "did the Sphero enter the requested 10x10 cm
square?" success criterion, the gesture-driven teleoperation
pipeline achieves a 94.3 % success rate over 174 deduplicated
trials and 16 sessions. The success rate is spatially uniform
across the workspace, with no region falling below 85 % and the
per-region variation explained by the small samples in the
extremes. The arrived and missed populations are cleanly separated
in robot-to-centre distance (means 5.5 cm vs. 34.5 cm with no
overlap), so the binary success label is robust. The few observed
failures correspond to upstream pointing errors that resolved to
the wrong grid cell or to user gestures arriving before the
previous approach had completed, rather than to any deficiency of
the navigation controller. The system is therefore well-suited as a
coarse tele-pointing interface, and further gains in reliability
are most likely to come from improvements upstream of navigation.
